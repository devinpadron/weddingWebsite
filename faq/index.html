Italy

Trains, driving, money, water, coffee, and a few customs worth knowing. None of this is a test; Italians are warm and forgiving of visitors.

What should I pack for my trip?+
  • A Type F/L plug adapter, and a proper voltage converter for any hair tools (see plugs below).
  • Any prescription medication in its original packaging, with enough for the trip.
  • Comfortable walking shoes. Historic towns are cobblestones and hills.
  • A light scarf or wrap to cover shoulders in churches.
  • A refillable water bottle.
  • Your passport, driving licence, and International Driving Permit if you’re renting a car.
How do trains work in Italy?+

Types of train. Frecciarossa and Frecciargento are Trenitalia’s high-speed trains between big cities, and Italo is a private company running similar ones. Intercity trains are slower and cheaper. Regionale and Regionale Veloce are the local trains, and these are the ones that stop at Fabro-Ficulle.

Buying tickets. Use trenitalia.com or the Trenitalia app, or the machines at any station (they have an English option). Italo sells its own tickets at italotreno.com. High-speed tickets come with a reserved seat and get cheaper the earlier you book. Regional tickets are a fixed price, so you can buy them the same day.

Validating. If you buy a regional ticket in the app, it’s tied to the train you chose and validates itself at departure. If the app shows a check-in prompt, tap it before you board. If you buy a paper regional ticket, you must stamp it in the small green and white machine on the platform before boarding, or you’ll be fined.

At the station. Your platform (binario) appears on the departures board, usually 10 to 15 minutes before the train leaves. On high-speed trains, find your carriage (carrozza) number on the ticket. Keep your ticket handy; the conductor checks it on board.

Strikes. Train strikes (sciopero) happen and are announced in advance. If you’re travelling on a strike day, check Trenitalia for which trains still run.

Anything I should know about driving?+

Be careful in historic town centres. The roads are tight and parking is scarce. Park outside the old town and walk in.

Many centres are also ZTL zones (limited traffic). There’s no barrier, just a camera that reads your plate, and the fine arrives through your rental company months later. If you see a red-circle ZTL sign, don’t drive past it.

Country roads are narrow and winding but beautiful. Take your time, and expect locals to pass you.

Cash or card, and what currency?+

Italy uses the euro (€). Right now, 1 US dollar is about €0.90. Cards (including contactless and phone payments) are widely accepted in cities and most restaurants, but carry some cash for small cafés, markets, taxis and rural spots. Withdraw euros from a bank ATM (a bancomat) rather than the airport exchange desks for a better rate, and always choose to be charged in euros, not dollars, to avoid a poor conversion.

Avoid the scammy exchange shops and anyone offering to exchange your money for cash. They will rip you off.

Do I need to tip?+

Tipping in Italy is modest and optional, nothing like the expectation in the US. Restaurants often add a small per-person coperto (cover charge) and sometimes servizio (service); check the bill. For coffee at the bar, locals may leave small change. There’s no need to tip 15–20%.

Will my phone work? What about data?+

Most modern phones work on Italian networks. Check your carrier’s roaming rates before you travel, as they can add up. A cheaper option is an eSIM (Airalo, Holafly and similar) you can install before you land, or a local SIM (TIM, Vodafone, WindTre) from a shop in Rome. Coverage is generally good, though it can thin out in the deep countryside.

What are the plugs and voltage?+

Italy runs on 230V and uses Type F and Type L sockets, so bring a European adapter. Phone and laptop chargers handle 230V automatically. Hair dryers, straighteners and curling irons are the problem. Most US ones are single-voltage and can blow (and blow the fuse) without a proper voltage converter, not just a plug adapter. Either bring a converter rated for heat tools, buy a dual-voltage tool, or borrow the hair dryer in your room.

What about water?+

Tap water is safe to drink, and public fountains in towns are free and clean. For bottled water we like Acqua Panna, Volvic, Levissima and Sant’Anna.

In restaurants, you’ll be asked still or sparkling (naturale or frizzante); it’s bottled and you’ll be charged for it. Ice isn’t commonplace either, so if you want it in your drink you’ll need to ask.

What if I need a pharmacy?+

Pharmacies (farmacia, marked by a green cross) are everywhere, and pharmacists can advise on minor ailments. Bring any prescription medication in its original packaging.

For everything else, buy it in Italy. Italian pharmacies carry excellent health care and skincare, and Samantha prefers the Italian brands over the American ones. Pack light and shop here.

Where should I shop for food?+

Go to Conad or Esselunga, the big supermarkets, and to the local markets whenever you can; the produce, cheese and bread are the point of being here. Skip the small corner minimarkets: the prices are higher and the quality is lower.

At markets and shops, look for the authentic store owners and local Italian businesses.

Do I need a visa?+

No. US citizens can visit Italy for up to 90 days without one.

How do I order coffee like a local?+

A caffè means an espresso: small, strong, and usually drunk standing at the bar in a few sips.

  • Latte means milk. Order one and you’ll get a glass of milk. If you want a typical latte, ask for a caffè latte.
  • Cappuccino is a morning drink. Ordering one after lunch marks you instantly as a tourist; locals switch to espresso after a meal.
  • It’s often cheaper standing at the bar (al banco) than sitting at a table. Pay at the till first if the bar asks.
  • Coffee to go barely exists. The pleasure is a short pause at the counter.
How do I beat jet lag?+

Try as hard as possible to sleep on the flight. When you land, do not take a nap; push through until the time you’d usually go to bed. This helps avoid jet lag for the rest of the trip. Try to schedule a full rest day for the day after you arrive, since you’ll be very tired.

Travelling back, do the opposite: stay up through the flight and don’t nap until it’s bedtime at home.

What is aperitivo?+

Make time for it. Aperitivo is the Italian tradition of a pre-dinner drink, usually between 6:00pm and 8:00pm, with a few snacks on the house: olives, chips, little sandwiches. Order a spritz, a glass of wine, or a Negroni, find a seat outside, and watch the town go by. It’s time to sit and enjoy where you are. Don’t fill up; dinner is still coming.

When do Italians eat, and when is everything closed?+
  • Lunch is usually 1:00–3:00pm. Dinner rarely starts before 8:00pm, and kitchens in smaller towns often don’t open until then.
  • Many shops and businesses close for a midday riposo (roughly 1:00–4:00pm), especially in small towns. Plan errands for the morning or late afternoon.
Anything I should know about dining out?+
  • The bill won’t come until you ask for it (il conto, per favore). Lingering is expected, not rushed.
  • A coperto (small cover charge per person) is normal and not a scam.
  • Meals unfold in courses at an unhurried pace. That’s the point. Settle in.
How should I dress for churches and towns?+

To enter churches, shoulders and knees should be covered, so carry a light scarf or wrap. Beyond that, smart-casual serves you well almost everywhere, and beachwear stays at the pool.

A few useful phrases?+
  • Buongiorno: good morning / hello (until afternoon)
  • Buonasera: good evening
  • Per favore / Grazie: please / thank you
  • Prego: you’re welcome / please go ahead
  • Scusi: excuse me
  • Il conto, per favore: the bill, please
  • Un caffè, per favore: an espresso, please

A simple buongiorno when you walk into a shop or café goes a long way.

Any etiquette I shouldn’t miss?+
  • Greetings matter: buongiorno on arrival and arrivederci on leaving. Among friends, a light kiss on both cheeks is common.
  • Life moves at a gentler pace. Lean into it rather than against it.
  • Keep your voice down in churches, and always ask before photographing people.
